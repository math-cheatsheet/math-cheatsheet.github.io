A brief history

How mathematics grew up

Six eras that reshaped what it means to count, measure, and prove.

c. 3000 – 500 BCE

Ancient Civilisations

Babylonian base-60 arithmetic, Egyptian geometry for surveying the Nile, and the earliest place-value systems. Mathematics was practical — but the seeds of abstraction were already being planted.

c. 600 BCE – 300 CE

Greek Deduction

Euclid's Elements turned mathematics into a deductive science. Archimedes pushed toward calculus, and the Pythagoreans discovered that not everything could be expressed as a ratio of whole numbers.

c. 500 – 1400 CE

The Islamic Golden Age & Medieval Europe

Algebra (al-jabr), the Hindu–Arabic numeral system, and the first systematic treatments of equations. Knowledge flowed across Baghdad, Córdoba, and eventually the universities of Europe.

17th Century

The Rise of Calculus

Newton and Leibniz independently invented calculus, giving humanity a language for change. Analytic geometry (Descartes) married algebra to curves, and the Scientific Revolution found its mathematical engine.

19th Century

The Birth of Rigor

Cauchy, Weierstrass, and Riemann put analysis on a firm logical footing. Non-Euclidean geometry shattered old certainties, and abstract algebra began to emerge as a field of its own.

20th Century – Today

The Modern Era

Hilbert's program, Gödel's incompleteness theorems, the Bourbaki collective, and the rise of computation. Mathematics now underpins cryptography, physics, machine learning, and the search for a unified theory of everything.

Mathematician of the Month

The Prince of Mathematicians

A child prodigy who became the most influential mathematician of the 19th century — and still shapes how we teach, measure, and prove today.

April 2025

Carl Friedrich Gauss

1777 – 1855 · Brunswick / Göttingen

Johann Carl Friedrich Gauss was a German polymath whose work spanned number theory, algebra, analysis, geometry, statistics, astronomy, and magnetism. Known as the “Prince of Mathematicians,” he was famously reluctant to publish incomplete results — leaving a treasure trove of ideas for later generations to rediscover.

  • Proved the construction of the regular heptadecagon (17-sided polygon) using only compass and straightedge — the first advance in regular polygon construction in over 2,000 years.
  • Introduced the triple-bar symbol (≡) for congruence and wrote Disquisitiones Arithmeticae, a foundational text in number theory.
  • Developed the method of least squares and the normal (Gaussian) distribution, now indispensable in statistics and machine learning.
  • Pioneered non-Euclidean geometry, Gaussian curvature, and the Theorema Egregium — reshaping differential geometry.
  • More than 100 mathematical and scientific concepts are named after him, from Gaussian elimination to the Gauss gravitational constant.
“Mathematics is the queen of the sciences, and number theory is the queen of mathematics.”
The pantheon

Other giants of the craft

A quick visual roll-call of minds who changed the rules.

Euclid

c. 300 BCE

Father of geometry; his Elements remained a standard textbook for over 2,000 years.

Isaac Newton

1642 – 1727

Co-inventor of calculus, formulated the laws of motion and universal gravitation.

Leonhard Euler

1707 – 1783

The most prolific mathematician in history; introduced much of modern notation.

Bernhard Riemann

1826 – 1866

Founder of Riemannian geometry; his zeta function remains central to the deepest open problem in maths.

David Hilbert

1862 – 1943

Set the agenda for 20th-century mathematics with his 23 problems.
